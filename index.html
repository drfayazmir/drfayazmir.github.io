About me
Hi, I'm Fayaz.

I study sleep, but I arrived there through the brainstem — and I have not really left it.

My doctoral work at Jawaharlal Nehru University, with Professor Sushil K. Jha, began with a narrow question that opened onto a much wider one: how does the brain's chemosensory machinery decide when REM sleep is permitted? Working in the locus coeruleus and the nucleus tractus solitarius, I found that acid-sensing ion channels and inwardly rectifying potassium channels do more than report CO₂. They gate the NREM-to-REM transition itself.

State regulation and metabolic sensing are not two parallel systems. They look more like one system, read at different timescales.

That conclusion is what moved me toward bioenergetics.

At Massachusetts General Hospital, in Dr. Christa Nehs's Sleep and Metabolism Laboratory, I record extracellular ATP with GRAB-ATP1.0 alongside chronic EEG/EMG polysomnography in freely behaving mice. That combination lets me ask what each vigilance state actually costs the brain, and how that cost shifts with age, with ketogenic diet, and in the presence of amyloid. The working hypothesis — ATP dynamics, sleep architecture, and glymphatic clearance as one coupled loop — is still a hypothesis, and parts of it will turn out to be wrong. The parts I can test, I am testing.

Most of what I do day to day is building. Surgeries, viral vectors, fiber placements, and the MATLAB pipelines that turn a night of photometry and EEG into scored, aligned, analyzable signal. I have spent as many hours debugging artifact rejection as designing experiments, and I stopped treating that as a distraction from the science some time ago. The pipeline is the instrument.

My training is entirely in rodents. I am clear-eyed about what that means for translation, and I think the honest version of this work is more useful to a collaborator than the inflated one.

Almost everything that mattered in my career started with someone taking a chance before the evidence was in — an advisor who handed me real responsibility early, a mentor who answered a persistent email, colleagues who argued with me until an idea got better. That is the kind of colleague I try to be.

This website exists because a CV says what I have done without saying how I think. If something here lines up with a question you are working on, I would like to hear about it.

Postdoctoral research

What does the brain actually spend during sleep, and what does that spending buy?

Sleep is expensive to stay in and expensive to lose, but the energetic accounting has mostly been inferred rather than measured in real time. In Dr. Nehs's lab I record extracellular ATP with GRAB-ATP1.0 simultaneously with EEG and EMG, so state and substrate are on the same clock. The questions I care about are what the ATP signature of each vigilance state looks like, how it degrades in aging and amyloid-bearing mice, and whether interventions that shift brain fuel use — ketogenic diet among them — move sleep architecture and clearance in the same direction.

Doctoral thesis

How does the brainstem's chemosensory system decide when REM sleep is allowed?

REM sleep suspends much of the body's homeostatic defense, which makes the decision to enter it a risky one. My thesis asked whether the same brainstem nuclei that monitor CO₂ also hold the gate. Microinjection and pharmacological blockade in the locus coeruleus and nucleus tractus solitarius showed that acid-sensing ion channels and Kir channels both alter REM output and the NREM-to-REM transition — evidence that chemosensing is not merely permissive background but part of the state-switching machinery itself.

What drives me

Questions that keep me curious

Brainstem pH/CO₂ sensors across LC, NTS and RTN, and how each couples acid–base signalling to neuronal excitability and sleep architecture.
●
Bioenergetics

Is sleep's benefit a matter of what the brain saves, or what it is able to spend?

The restorative account treats sleep as recovery from an energy deficit. But slow-wave sleep is also when some of the most expensive cellular work happens. I want to know which framing the ATP data actually support, and whether the answer differs by region.

●
Neurodegeneration

Does sleep disruption precede amyloid pathology, follow it, or feed it?

All three have support, which usually means the question is badly posed. Longitudinal recordings in the same animals, before and through pathology, are the only way I know to separate them.

●
Circuits

Why does the locus coeruleus fail so early, and so reliably?

Its vulnerability is well documented and poorly explained. Long unmyelinated projections, high metabolic load, and sustained activity during wake are all plausible contributors — and all testable against the LC's energetic profile across the sleep–wake cycle.

●
Translation

What would it take to make a rodent sleep finding worth a clinical trial?

My work is entirely in rodents, and I would rather confront that gap than talk around it. The question is which measures transfer — spectral features, state transitions, metabolic markers — and which are artifacts of a small brain on a short day.

Scientific Adventures

"Industry and academia are asking many of the same questions. They are not asking them at the same speed."

BioMed X, with Biogen, ran a global call for one group leader and two postdoctoral positions, and brought fifteen of us to New Haven for the boot camp that decides it — five group leaders and ten postdocs. The organising question was how the nucleus tractus solitarius shapes autoimmune disease through the peripheral inflammatory reflex arc. For someone who has spent years arguing that the NTS is a sleep and chemosensory structure, being asked to think of it as an immune controller was a genuinely useful dislocation.

The format was demanding. Everyone presented their own work orally on arrival, and then we were split into five teams — one group leader with two postdocs each — and given the job of building a defensible research proposal from scratch. Senior scientists from BioMed X and Biogen sat in as coaches throughout, which meant the feedback arrived continuously rather than at the end.

What I took away was less about immunology than about pace. Industry runs on a different clock, and a proposal that would read as appropriately hedged in an academic setting reads as indecisive across the table from a drug developer. Learning to present the same data with that audience in mind sharpened how I argue generally. The networking was the obvious benefit; the coaching was the lasting one.

Exploring Places

"A new place rearranges how you think before you notice it has."

I moved from Delhi to Boston, and the first New England autumn genuinely stopped me. Nothing had prepared me for a whole street turning gold in the space of a week, or for how quickly it then goes.

Weekends usually mean walking somewhere I have not been — a trail, a coastline, a town I picked because the name looked interesting on a map. Coffee in hand, no particular plan, which is the part I like. Some of my better thinking about an experiment has happened three miles into a walk with no notebook anywhere nearby.

New England is unreasonably good for this. There is always another corner of it I have not seen yet.

Love for Nature Clicks

"Photography taught me to look twice before deciding what is there."

I photograph almost entirely on my phone, almost entirely by accident. Something in the light catches, and I stop.

Most of what I keep is seasonal — blossom against brick, pumpkins stacked under a tree that has no business flowering in October, the particular flat blue the harbour turns on a clear autumn morning. Boston gives you all four seasons and does not let you ignore any of them.

There is an obvious overlap with the day job that I did not plan. Both are about noticing a small change against a noisy background, and both get better the more patient you are willing to be about waiting for the right moment.

Me: A Photogenic

"Somehow I am always the one in front of the lens, and I have stopped resisting it."

I should admit something. For a person who spends most of his time photographing trees and water, I end up in a surprising number of frames myself.

Friends find this funny. I have been told, more than once, that I have exactly one good angle and that I have learned it thoroughly. This is probably fair.

I include it here because a website full of hypnograms and ion channels gives a slightly incomplete picture. I enjoy a good photograph, I enjoy being in one, and I would rather say so than pretend to a seriousness I do not actually maintain outside the lab.
